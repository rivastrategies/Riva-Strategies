Selling a House With Code Violations or Unfinished Repairs

Prepare a southeast Houston property with code notices, unfinished additions, or repair concerns for a possible as-is sale, including Pasadena, Deer Park, and La Porte resources.

By Lone Star Property Solutions · Updated November 9, 2026

The useful starting point

Describe the property’s actual condition, identify known notices or unfinished work, and ask the responsible local department and appropriate advisers about unresolved requirements. An as-is purchase proposal does not by itself clear a violation or establish that work is permitted.

In this guide1. Separate observations from official findings2. Confirm the responsible local department3. Prepare a safe evidence package4. Put responsibilities into the proposed terms

1. Separate observations from official findings

A cracked wall, an unfinished room, a contractor estimate, and a city notice are different kinds of information. Identify what you observed and what another party documented. Do not guess at the legal or structural significance of a condition.

  • Describe the affected area and known work.
  • State whether a notice or inspection record exists.
  • Mark missing permits or plans as unknown.

2. Confirm the responsible local department

Pasadena, Deer Park, and La Porte maintain their own building resources. Use the actual jurisdiction and proposed work when asking questions. A nearby city’s requirements or a postal address alone may not answer what applies.

  • Start with the exact street address.
  • Ask the local department about available records.
  • Use qualified professionals for safety and repair assessments.

3. Prepare a safe evidence package

Available exterior photos, room views, estimates, and project notes can help a buyer understand the scope. Do not enter an unsafe structure, climb a roof, or disturb suspect materials to gather images.

  • Show the same area from a safe viewpoint.
  • Label work as completed, unfinished, or not evaluated.
  • Remove personal information from documents and photographs.

4. Put responsibilities into the proposed terms

Discuss outstanding work, notices, access, and any proposed responsibility before signing. A buyer’s willingness to review a property does not establish that a transfer is feasible or that a particular repair can be skipped.

  • Share known constraints early.
  • Confirm transaction questions with appropriate professionals.
  • Compare price with costs, conditions, and unresolved work.

Questions Homeowners Ask

Can I submit a property with a code notice?+

Yes. Describe the notice and current condition. A purchase remains subject to review; the inquiry does not resolve the notice.

Does selling as-is remove a code violation?+

Do not assume so. Ask the responsible department and your advisers what remains required and review responsibilities in the proposed agreement.

Should I finish an addition first?+

You can explore a sale before starting more work. Ask about permits, safety, costs, and your options before committing to a renovation.

What if I cannot find the permits?+

Say the records are unavailable. Ask the responsible department about a records search instead of describing the work as permitted without support.

Do I need a new inspection to submit?+

No new inspection is required for the initial inquiry. Share available information; the buyer may require further review.

Can I photograph a dangerous room?+

Do not enter unsafe areas for an inquiry. Describe the concern and explain that access or photographs are unavailable.
